# PART III: CLASSICAL BUDDHIST TRADITIONS ON MANY WORLDS

*Abhidharma, Yogācāra, Huayan, Tiantai, Vajrayāna, and Dzogchen*

## Introduction: How to Read These Traditions

Part II sketched a multi-dimensional worldview using the Middle Way as read by Tsongkhapa and Mipham. Buddhism, however, has thought about many worlds for more than two thousand years, in India, China, and Tibet. This Part surveys six of those traditions.

Two cautions apply throughout.

**They do not all say the same thing.** It is tempting to present every Buddhist school as a witness for one shared view. That would be inaccurate. Some of these traditions hold positions that the Middle Way explicitly criticizes; others use language that a careful Madhyamaka reader would want to qualify. Each section below therefore asks three questions: What does this tradition say about many worlds? Where does it agree with the view of Parts I and II? And where does it differ, or need care?

**They are not physics.** These traditions describe worlds in terms of karma, perception, meditation, and awakening. They are not early versions of modern cosmology, and they did not anticipate modern physics. Where they resemble Part II, the resemblance is in the shape of the reasoning, not in any scientific content.

The traditions are taken roughly in historical order.

## 1. Abhidharma: The Map of the Realms

### A. What It Says

The Abhidharma ("higher teaching") traditions organized the Buddha's teachings into systematic lists and maps. The most influential summary is Vasubandhu's *Abhidharmakośa* (the *Treasury of Abhidharma*), written from the standpoint of the Sarvāstivāda and Sautrāntika schools, before Vasubandhu's turn to Yogācāra.

Its third chapter lays out the cosmos in detail:

- **Three realms:** the desire realm (which contains hell beings, hungry ghosts, animals, humans, and the lower gods), the form realm of subtle meditative gods, and the formless realm of pure meditative absorption.
- **A vertical geography:** Mount Meru at the center, four continents around it, the god realms rising above, and the hells lying beneath. The main abode of the hungry ghosts, the realm of Yama, is placed far beneath our continent, though some hungry ghosts are also said to wander among humans.
- **Two kinds of world:** the *sattva-loka* (the world of beings) and the *bhājana-loka* (the "vessel world," their shared environment).

Crucially, the fourth chapter opens with the statement that the variety of the world is born of action (*karmajaṃ lokavaicitryam*). The vessel world is not a pre-existing box into which beings are placed. It is formed by the shared karma of the beings who will live in it, and it decays when that karma is exhausted.

### B. Where It Agrees

The Abhidharma already holds the key idea of Part II: worlds are shaped by the actions and habits of the beings who inhabit them. Even the "vessel" is a product of shared karma, not an independent container.

### C. Where It Differs

The Abhidharma map is strongly spatial. It describes realms stacked above and below one another, separated by measured distances. Its philosophy also differs from the Middle Way: the Sarvāstivāda school held that the ultimate building blocks of experience, the *dharmas*, have their own intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*). That is precisely the belief Nāgārjuna set out to refute.

Read in the light of Part II, the Abhidharma map is best taken as a conventional map that serves meditation, ethics, and the understanding of rebirth. It is not a description of an absolute space.

## 2. Yogācāra: Worlds as Shared Ripening

### A. What It Says

The Yogācāra ("practice of yoga") school, founded by Asaṅga and Vasubandhu in the fourth and fifth centuries, explains shared worlds through consciousness.

- **Shared perception without an external object.** As Part I described, Vasubandhu's *Twenty Verses* (*Viṃśatikā*) uses the hungry ghosts who all see the same river of pus to show that shared perception does not require an external object. Beings with similar karma experience similar worlds.
- **The storehouse consciousness.** Yogācāra explains this through the *ālaya-vijñāna*, a deep layer of consciousness that carries karmic seeds from life to life. Asaṅga's *Compendium of the Mahāyāna* (*Mahāyānasaṃgraha*) distinguishes "common" seeds, which ripen as the shared environment, from "uncommon" seeds, which ripen as each being's own body and senses.
- **One thing, many seeings.** Yogācāra texts associated with Asaṅga also use the example of one thing seen differently by hungry ghosts, animals, humans, and gods: an early form of the bowl example.

### B. Where It Agrees

Yogācāra gives the most detailed classical account of *why* beings of one kind share a world. Its idea of common and uncommon seeds explains both the overlap and the difference between worlds, much as Part II's account of shared and unshared channels does.

### C. Where It Differs

Yogācāra grounds these shared worlds in the storehouse consciousness. As Part I, §2D explained, Candrakīrti rejected this move: if the external object is not established by itself, neither is the mind that perceives it. Making consciousness the foundation is, from the Middle Way perspective, simply another floor.

Two qualifications are fair to Yogācāra. First, some modern scholars read Yogācāra less as a metaphysical idealism ("only mind exists") and more as an analysis of how experience is constructed. Second, later Indian thinkers, notably Śāntarakṣita, combined Yogācāra and Madhyamaka, accepting Yogācāra conventionally while denying that consciousness is ultimately real. On either reading, Yogācāra is best treated here as a valuable partner in the conversation, and on the decisive question of a foundation, as a contrast rather than an ally.

## 3. Huayan: The Non-Obstruction of Phenomena

### A. What It Says

The Huayan ("Flower Ornament") school of China takes its name from the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, a vast scripture filled with images of worlds within worlds. In the sūtra, buddha-fields as numerous as atoms appear within a single dust mote, and no world crowds out another.

Huayan thinkers developed these images into philosophy:

- **Dushun** (557–640), traditionally counted as the first patriarch, is credited with the *Contemplation of the Dharmadhātu*, which leads the practitioner from emptiness, through the non-obstruction of principle and phenomena, to the all-pervading inclusion of everything in everything.
- **Fazang** (643–712), the school's great systematizer, explained mutual inclusion through images such as the golden lion and the hall of mirrors, and through the image of **Indra's Net**: a net with a jewel at every knot, in which each jewel reflects all the others, and none is the central or original jewel.
- **Chengguan** (738–839) set out the teaching of the **Four Dharmadhātus**, the four ways of seeing reality:
  1. **Shi (事):** the realm of distinct, everyday phenomena.
  2. **Li (理):** the realm of principle, or emptiness.
  3. **Li-shi wu'ai (理事無礙):** the non-obstruction of principle and phenomena: form is emptiness, and emptiness is form.
  4. **Shi-shi wu'ai (事事無礙):** the non-obstruction of phenomena with one another.

The fourth view is the key one for many worlds. Because no phenomenon has an independent self-nature, phenomena do not exclude one another. Each depends on all the others, and so, in Huayan's language, each contains all the others.

### B. Where It Agrees

Huayan is the clearest classical statement that worlds do not obstruct one another *because* they are empty. That is exactly the reasoning of Part II: worlds that have no solid, independent core have nothing with which to push each other away. Indra's Net, in which no jewel is the original, is a fine image for Part II's "no master world."

### C. Where It Needs Care

Two points need care.

- **Non-obstruction is not a claim about space.** The "dust mote containing infinite realms" is not a statement that countless worlds are packed into one tiny location of a shared space. It expresses mutual dependence: each thing is what it is only in relation to everything else. Reading it spatially would bring back the container of Part I, §7.
- **The status of "principle."** Huayan also teaches that phenomena arise from the one true nature (*xingqi*, 性起), closely linked to buddha-nature. Some readers, ancient and modern, have taken this "principle" as a positive, all-embracing ground. A Middle Way reading insists that principle is emptiness, which is not a ground of anything. Huayan can be read either way, and this essay adopts the second reading.

## 4. Tiantai: The Mutual Inclusion of the Ten Realms

### A. What It Says

The Tiantai school, systematized by **Zhiyi** (538–597), builds on Nāgārjuna directly. Its teaching of the **Three Truths** (emptiness, the provisional, and the middle) develops a famous verse of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (24.18), which says that dependent arising is emptiness, is a dependent designation, and is the middle way.

Tiantai's teaching on worlds has two parts:

- **The mutual inclusion of the ten realms** (*shijie huju*, 十界互具). There are ten realms of existence: hell beings, hungry ghosts, animals, demigods (*asuras*), humans, and gods (the six realms of rebirth), together with *śrāvakas*, *pratyekabuddhas*, bodhisattvas, and buddhas. Each realm contains all ten. A human contains the potential of hell and the potential of buddhahood; a hell being is not cut off from awakening.
- **Three thousand realms in a single thought-moment** (*yinian sanqian*, 一念三千). In Zhiyi's *Great Calming and Contemplation* (*Mohe zhiguan*), the ten realms, each containing ten, multiplied by further factors, give three thousand aspects of existence, all present in a single moment of mind.

### B. Where It Agrees

Tiantai makes many worlds immediate and personal. The realms are not remote places; they are possibilities present in each moment of experience. Which one becomes vivid depends on habit, intention, and practice. This matches Part II's conclusion that transformation is a change in seeing, not a change in location.

### C. Where It Needs Care

- **A thought-moment is not a place.** The "three thousand realms in a single thought-moment" concern a moment of experience, not a point in space. They should not be read as saying that a hell and a pure land occupy the same spatial location.
- **"Inherent" inclusion.** Later Tiantai, especially in the Song dynasty, emphasized that the Buddha *inherently includes* (*xingju*, 性具) even the hell realms and evil. This bold teaching is distinctive to Tiantai and is stronger than anything the Indian Middle Way asserts. It is valuable as a remedy for treating buddhahood as a distant, separate realm, but it is Tiantai's own position, not a general Buddhist one.

## 5. Vajrayāna: Pure Vision and the Mandala

### A. What It Says

In the Vajrayāna traditions of India and Tibet, the theme of many worlds becomes a method of practice. Its root is already present in the Mahāyāna sūtras. In the first chapter of the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*, Śāriputra wonders why the Buddha's land looks so impure, full of stones, thorns, and filth. The Buddha touches the ground with his toe, and the whole world appears as a jewelled pure land. The Buddha explains that the land was always pure; Śāriputra had not seen it. As the sūtra says, when the mind is pure, the buddha-field is pure.

Vajrayāna develops this into the practice of **pure vision** (Tibetan *dag snang*):

- In meditation, the practitioner trains in seeing the environment as the mandala palace of the deity, beings as deities, sounds as mantra, and thoughts as wisdom.
- The elements are recognized as wisdom energies, often personified as female buddhas. In the Nyingma tradition, the water element is Māmakī, as in the bowl example of Part I.
- This is understood not as pretending, but as training to see what the Vimalakīrti story says was always there.

### B. Where It Agrees

Pure vision is Part II's "change in seeing, not in location" put into practice. Nothing needs to be moved, rebuilt, or traveled to. What changes is the way the same field of experience is met, as in Mipham's account of pure conventional valid knowing (Part I, §4D).

### C. Where It Needs Care

- **Not all pure lands are described as "here."** Many Mahāyāna sources describe pure lands as real places in other directions; Amitābha's Sukhāvatī, for example, is said to lie countless buddha-fields to the west. Vajrayāna and the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* emphasize that purity is a matter of perception, but they do not simply deny the traditional descriptions. Both ways of speaking coexist within Buddhism.
- **This essay describes a view, not a method.** In Vajrayāna, pure vision is cultivated within a lineage, with empowerment and a qualified teacher. Nothing here is meant as practice instruction.

## 6. Dzogchen: Unimpeded Openness

### A. What It Says

In the Dzogchen (Great Perfection) teachings of Longchenpa (1308–1364) and Ju Mipham (1846–1912), described in Part I, §4, the basis is empty in essence (*ka dag*, primordial purity) and spontaneously present in nature (*lhun grub*). Appearances are the expressive power or dynamism (*rtsal*) of the basis, not products manufactured by it.

Dzogchen texts characterize this openness as *zang thal*: unimpeded, transparent, all-penetrating. Because no appearance has a solid core, appearances do not block one another. The many worlds of beings can all arise without crowding anything, because the basis is not an extended physical space that could fill up. It is not a space at all.

### B. Where It Agrees

Dzogchen, as read in Part I, is the closest of these traditions to the view of this essay. Its insistence that the basis is a "ground" only conventionally, that awareness is not a projector, and that display is not causal production, protects the many-worlds view from sliding into either a cosmic container or a cosmic mind.

### C. Where It Needs Care

Dzogchen has long been controversial within Tibetan Buddhism. Critics have argued that its language of a "basis" or "ground" reintroduces a foundation, or resembles non-Buddhist doctrines of an eternal self. Longchenpa and Mipham answered these charges by insisting that the basis is empty and is called a ground only conventionally, which is the reading followed here. Readers should know, however, that this reading is defended, not simply assumed, within the tradition itself.

## 7. Reading the Traditions Together

The six traditions can be summarized as follows.

| Tradition | Key idea about many worlds | Where it agrees with this essay | Where it differs or needs care |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abhidharma** | Realms formed by the shared karma of their inhabitants (*karmajaṃ lokavaicitryam*) | The "vessel world" is shaped by beings, not a pre-existing box | Strongly spatial map; holds that *dharmas* have their own intrinsic nature |
| **Yogācāra** | Shared worlds as the ripening of common karmic seeds | Detailed account of why beings share or do not share a world | Grounds worlds in the storehouse consciousness, which Candrakīrti rejected |
| **Huayan** | Non-obstruction of all phenomena; Indra's Net | Worlds do not obstruct one another because they are empty | Not a spatial claim; "principle" must not become a positive ground |
| **Tiantai** | Ten realms mutually included; three thousand realms in one thought-moment | Worlds are possibilities of experience, not distant places | A thought-moment is not a location; "inherent inclusion" is Tiantai's own bold claim |
| **Vajrayāna** | Pure vision; the world as mandala | Transformation is a change in seeing, not in location | Traditional pure lands also described as distant; a practice requiring a teacher |
| **Dzogchen** | Unimpeded openness (*zang thal*) of empty, spontaneously present appearances | Closest to this essay; no container and no cosmic mind | The "basis" is read as a ground only conventionally, a reading the tradition itself has had to defend |

What do these traditions share, despite their differences? Three things stand out.

1. **Worlds are shaped by beings.** In every tradition surveyed, the realms are shaped by the karma, perception, or realization of those who live in them. None treats the world as a ready-made stage that exists the same way for everyone.
2. **Worlds need not obstruct one another.** From Huayan's Indra's Net to Dzogchen's unimpeded openness, the absence of a solid core is what lets many worlds arise without crowding.
3. **Transformation is a change in seeing.** From the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* to Tiantai and pure vision, liberation is presented not as travel to a better place, but as meeting this same field of experience without distortion.

What they do not share is an answer to the question of a foundation. Abhidharma accepts intrinsically real *dharmas*; Yogācāra accepts a foundational consciousness; Huayan and Dzogchen can be read with or without a positive ground. The view of this essay chooses the reading without a foundation, following Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti, Tsongkhapa, and Mipham. That is a choice made for reasons, given in Part I, not a consensus the traditions already share.

The traditions, then, are not proofs of the multi-dimensional worldview, any more than physics was. They are a long conversation, carried on across many centuries and cultures, about the same puzzle with which this essay began: one bowl, many beings, and no hidden original beneath them.
