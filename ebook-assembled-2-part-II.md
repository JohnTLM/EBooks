# Part II: The Logics and the Substrates

## Chapter 3: Two Logics

Watch a candle burn for a minute. The flame leans, steadies, shrinks when the door opens, and recovers. Ask what is happening, and the answer arrives before any thought: the flame is changing. Something is there, and something is happening to it.

Even after the arguments of Part I have been followed and accepted, this picture tends to return the moment attention is off duty. One still sees a something undergoing change. The persistence of that picture is the subject of this chapter. It is not a stray belief that a counter-argument can remove. It is a way of reasoning. Call it the logic of substance. It starts with things and then asks what happens to them. Do they arise, last, move, end, stay the same?

The analysis of this book starts one step earlier. It does not begin with things. It asks how anything is identified as a thing in the first place, and it finds that it is identified only in dependence: on parts, causes, contrasts and the designation under which it is picked out. No thing stands there before that identification. Call this the logic of dependency. The two logics use the same words, change, cause, identity, continuity and the path. But because they begin in different places, the same words lead them into different worlds.

### Do we actually think substantially?

Before the two logics can be compared, an honest question must be faced. Does anyone actually reason in the substantialist way? Ask people whether things have fixed essences and most will say no. Everything changes, they will say. Everything is connected. Nothing lasts. Many have read enough to call themselves process thinkers. If the logic of substance is a position nobody holds, this chapter is arguing with a ghost.

The test is not what people say when asked, but what they do when they are not asked. Three places show it.

*In space.* Asked, few people will say that space is a thing. Yet almost everyone pictures it as one: a vast, empty room in which objects are placed, which would remain if every star were removed. Newton wrote the intuition into physics, holding that absolute space stays always the same, without relation to anything outside it. The picture shapes casual speech: there is room for a sofa, nothing between the stars. What is never met is space apart from distance and reach. The room that would survive the removal of everything is not found. It is assumed.

*In time.* Time is held more tightly still. It is pictured as a river flowing on its own, carrying events whether or not anything happens. Language treats time as a substance in two ways at once. It is a moving thing: the deadline approaches, the years went by. And it is a stuff: we spend it, save it, and run out of it, the pattern Lakoff and Johnson called "time is money". Cultures differ in the shape of the picture, and speakers of Aymara, Núñez and Sweetser found, place the past in front and the future behind. But the substance remains: a time that is somewhere, apart from the events placed in it. The general anaesthetic of Chapter 9 is the plain counter-evidence. Three hours pass on the clock and none in experience. No river was found that flowed by itself.

*In grammar.* Every sentence about change has a subject and a predicate. The flame flickers, the market falls, I have changed. The subject is held still so that the predicate can happen to it. This is not a theory anyone chose. It is the shape a thought takes before it becomes a thought. Saying that everything changes does not escape it, since "everything" becomes the subject and is still pictured as the thing doing the changing.

The answer, then, is yes, but with a qualification that matters. We do not think substantially all the time. Much of practical life already runs on relations. A cook judges dough by how it responds, not by its essence. A driver reads the traffic, not the cars in themselves. The habit is a default that switches on at particular places: when we explain and when we name. Those are the places where the logic of substance does its work, and they are where the logic of dependency has to be tested.

### What "logic" means here

"Logic" has a precise sense in this book, and it is easy to hear it in another. It does not mean that Madhyamaka has a special logic of its own, and it does not mean that emptiness is reached by going beyond logic. It means the necessity of a consequence. Whoever proposes that something exists from its own side must say what it is. To say what it is, they must pick it out by contrast and designation, and so make it dependent. The argument borrows its premises from the opponent and shows that they cannot be stated without undermining themselves.

Three features follow. The logic is not inductive: it does not survey the world and report that no essences have turned up. It shows what anyone proposing an exception must already be doing. Its conclusion is a negation without a thesis, a negation that removes inherent existence and installs nothing in its place. And it does not break the ordinary rules of reasoning. It relies on non-contradiction throughout. The chariot cannot be both identical with its parts and different from them. Fire cannot be both one with its fuel and other than it. The analysis has its force because it holds the opponent to exactly these rules.

What, then, of the famous refusals: neither existent nor non-existent, neither arising nor ceasing? These are not contradictions, and they do not suspend the law of excluded middle. They refuse a presupposition. "Is the present king of France bald?" cannot be answered yes or no, because there is no present king of France. The question fails before either answer could be true. "Does the self exist, or not?" and "Does this moment arise or cease?" fail in the same way. Each presupposes a bearer with a status of its own. When no such bearer is found, both answers fall together. Logic has not been suspended. The question had nothing to be about.

This is the difference between the Middle Way and its imitations. A view that claims to transcend logic can say anything, and so says nothing. The logic of dependency says one thing exactly: what is proposed as standing on its own is not found to do so, and the proposal undermines itself.

## Chapter 4: The Given

If there is no observer standing behind experience, the next question is whether appearance rests on something beneath it: some undesignated layer that is simply there, which concepts then describe. Philosophers have called this the Given. The idea has a long history in the West and an older, more rigorous one in Buddhist epistemology.

Begin with Locke. He divided qualities into primary ones, such as shape and motion, which belong to bodies themselves, and secondary ones, such as colour and warmth, which are only powers in bodies to produce sensations in us. The primary qualities were the bedrock. Wilfrid Sellars later attacked the whole structure from inside empiricism, arguing that the idea of a pure given in experience is a myth: what we call a sensation is already conceptual.

Buddhist philosophy had worked out its own version of the Given centuries earlier, and with greater rigour. Dignāga, in the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, opens with a symmetry. There are exactly two means of valid knowledge, perception and inference, because there are exactly two kinds of object. The particular, what characterises itself alone, is grasped by perception. The universal, a general character shared by many things, is grasped by inference and concept. Perception itself is defined as free from conceptual construction.

Dharmakīrti sharpened the scheme. Perception is free from construction and non-erroneous. What makes the particular ultimately real is that it alone can do something: it is capable of causal efficacy. Universals do nothing. The fire that is merely thought of burns no one.

The result is a two-storey epistemology. On the lower floor are momentary, efficacious particulars, touched by perception before any concept arrives. On the upper floor are concepts, which never touch particulars at all. They construct general characters by exclusion, so that "cow" means what is not non-cow, and they are useful precisely because, strictly speaking, they misrepresent what they seem to present.

This position should not be caricatured. Dharmakīrti does not posit a bare substance waiting beneath qualities. He rejects the split between substance and property altogether. His claim is subtler: the real is what functions, and what functions is met by perception before designation. That is why it is the strongest form of the Given. It is a Given without a substrate, built by philosophers who had already accepted momentariness and dependent arising. If any account could keep an undesignated layer while honouring everything else this book has argued, it would be this one.

### Candrakīrti's reply

In the first chapter of his commentary on the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, Candrakīrti answers a view closely resembling Dignāga's. Three of his moves bear directly on the Given.

The first concerns the word own-characteristic itself. A characteristic is the characteristic of something: hardness characterises earth. Is the characteristic different from what it characterises? Then earth, apart from its hardness, has no characteristic, and nothing has been picked out. Is it the same? Then nothing characterises anything, and "own-characteristic" says nothing. This is the analysis Nāgārjuna applied to space. Characteristic and characterised are designated together, and neither is found first. A particular singled out as "this, characterised by itself alone" has already been singled out by contrast, which is the very operation the two-storey scheme reserved for the upper floor.

The second move concerns what perception is of. Candrakīrti does not abolish perception. He accepts it as a means of knowledge, together with inference, testimony and analogy, as the world uses them. But what the world perceives is the pot, not a momentary patch of colour. The object of perception is already a designated whole, met as a pot from the start, in the way a cup on a table is seen as a cup.

The third move comes from Nāgārjuna. Means of knowledge and objects of knowledge, he argues, are not established by themselves. Each is established only in relation to the other. Dignāga's founding symmetry, two means because two objects, is therefore not a foundation. It is co-definition, like long and short.

Tsongkhapa makes the conclusion explicit and makes it a defining mark of the Prāsaṅgika view. Nothing is established by its own characteristic, not even conventionally. Other Buddhist schools refuted the particular's ultimate status and kept it conventionally. Prāsaṅgika refuses it at every level.

### What is kept, and what goes

The refusal is narrow. Valid cognition is kept. Perception is still distinguished from inference, a snake in a rope is still corrected by a lamp, and Tsongkhapa's criteria still sort sound conventions from mistaken ones. What goes is only the claim that perception's object is there from its own side, before designation, as the floor beneath it.

Dharmakīrti's deepest reason for that floor was efficacy. *Substrateless World* answers it. The key's power to open is found only together with the lock, the hand and the door. Causal efficacy is itself a relation. The mark that Dharmakīrti took for the stamp of independent reality turns out to be the clearest mark of dependence.

When the particular is not found, the two-storey building has no ground floor. Perception is not a lower layer, and concept is not a top coat. Vividness and designation are mingled through and through.

## Chapter 5: Conceptual All the Way Down?

If there is no Given, the opposite conclusion beckons. Perhaps experience is concept through and through, and the world is simply what our concepts make of it.

The most careful modern form of this thought is John McDowell's. In *Mind and World* he argued that conceptual capacities are already at work in perceptual experience itself, which is how experience can give us reasons. The conceptual, he wrote, is unbounded. There is no outer edge beyond which a non-conceptual given waits. Readers often summarise the view as "conceptual all the way down", though that is a gloss rather than McDowell's own wording.

Hubert Dreyfus objected that this cannot be right of skilled, absorbed activity. The chess master who sees the move, and the expert who simply copes, are not applying concepts at all. McDowell's reply is one of the most instructive exchanges of recent philosophy.

The Madhyamaka sides with neither. McDowell is no idealist. He insists the world constrains what we think. But a mind whose concepts reach everywhere, read as an account of what reality is, has replaced the substance of the realist with the concept of the idealist. *Substrateless World* met this move in the form of idealism, panpsychism and Mind-Only. Dreyfus's absorbed coping, read in the same way, restores a pre-conceptual floor. The Given reappears in practical rather than perceptual dress. Each makes one term prior.

What the analysis keeps is a symmetry, in two parts.

- **Appearance is not raw material for concepts.** The ice stings with its own sharpness, and no one decides the sting. A shadow cannot be designated a bridge. The constraint that keeps designation from being arbitrary comes from the vividness itself.
- **Concepts are not a veil over appearance.** The cup is seen as a cup, and the duck as a duck. Designation is not a screen held in front of the world. It is in the appearing.

Neither is the ground of the other. Concepts draw their content and their correction from what appears, and what appears is picked out and made usable through designation. They are designated together, like long and short. Even the words "conceptual" and "non-conceptual" are co-defined, each identified only as the other's contrast.

Dreyfus's own examples show this. Riding a bicycle is not a set of rules stored in the head, and it is not a wordless reflex beneath all rules. The skill is designated together with rider, machine, slope and wind, and found in none of them alone. And designation is wider than language. "Conceptual all the way down" mistakes designation for a kind of sentence. "Non-conceptual at bottom" mistakes the absence of words for the absence of designation.

The ordinary distinction survives. Seeing a snake is not remembering one, and a perception can correct a thought, as the lamp corrects the rope. Dharmakīrti's line between perception and conception keeps its conventional use. What it loses is the claim to mark two natures, one beneath the other.

So the answer to the objection is neither "conceptual all the way down" nor "non-conceptual at the bottom". There is no down.

## Chapter 6: Energy

A reader trained in physics may feel that a carrier has been found after all. Energy is conserved. It changes form, chemical into heat, heat into motion, mass into light, while the total stays the same. Surely energy is the something that persists through every change: the modern successor to substance.

Physics itself does not treat it so. Energy is not a fluid poured from one container into another. It is a quantity, a number computed in different ways for different forms, whose total for a closed system comes out the same before and after. Richard Feynman made the point in his lectures: physics has no picture of energy as blobs of anything, only a calculation that keeps balancing.

Three further facts confirm that energy is no substrate. Its amount depends on the frame of reference. A moving ball has kinetic energy relative to the ground and none relative to a runner keeping pace with it, so no amount belongs to the ball by itself. Its conservation follows, by Noether's theorem, from a symmetry: the laws of physics do not change over time. It is a feature of how regularities hang together, not a store of stuff. And in an expanding universe, where light is stretched and loses energy, there is no well-defined total that is conserved at all. A true substance could not simply fail to add up.

From the side of this book, "where did the energy come from, and where did it go?" is the extreme of coming and going asked of a number. When a match burns, chemical energy becomes heat and light. The sentence is correct, and the regularity it records is among the most reliable conventions there are. But nothing is found leaving the match head and travelling into the flame. Conservation is a designated regularity of the highest reliability. It is not a passenger.

*Notes: Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. 1, ch. 4. Noether, "Invariante Variationsprobleme", Nachrichten von der Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen (1918): 235–257; the chapter relies only on the time-translation symmetry.*

## Chapter 7: Potentiality and Plasticity

A subtler candidate remains. If nothing is fixed from its own side, appearances seem endlessly malleable. The same dress is seen as white and gold or as blue and black. The same lines are seen as a duck or a rabbit. The same electron appears as a particle or a wave depending on how it is asked. Surely, then, there is a store of potentialities from which these appearances are drawn: Aristotle's potency, Heisenberg's potentia, the abstract space of possibilities that quantum theory describes. Appearances would be the actualisations, and the potentialities what is really there.

This is the move that *Substrateless World*, in its treatment of quantum measurement, calls a substrate for emptiness. Seeing that phenomena have no fixed nature, the substantialist mind gives their openness a home behind them, and so turns emptiness into a new substance, one made of possibilities instead of stuff.

But a potential is identified only through its actualisation and its partner. Salt is soluble only in relation to water. A seed is a seed only in relation to the sprout. "Could" is a designation made within a framework of conditions, not a thing waiting backstage. When Nāgārjuna says that for whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible, the verb means "fits" or "works". It does not describe a reservoir.

Nor is the plasticity infinite. Appearances are open because they depend, and constrained for the same reason. A rope can appear as a snake in dim light. A shadow cannot be walked across as a bridge. The dress divided its viewers into two colourings, not into every colour at once. A plasticity independent of conditions would itself be a kind of own-nature, a power able to make anything from its own side. That is the idealist extreme, not the Middle Way. Appearances are exactly as plastic as their conditions allow, and no more.

The Dzogchen word *ma 'gags pa*, unobstructed, fits better than "potential". Nothing blocks the display, and there is no store behind it from which the display comes.

## Chapter 8: Continuity Without a Carrier

If there is no carrier, what makes a life one life, a habit one habit, a river one river? The logic of substance has only one answer: something persists. A soul, a stream of consciousness, a neural core, or the energy that is conserved: each is proposed as what carries continuity through time. Remove it, the substantialist expects, and what is left is a series of disconnected flashes, each moment sealed off from the next.

The logic of dependency rejects both pictures, the carrier and the disconnection. Nāgasena's lamp is the classic image. The flame of the last watch of the night is neither the flame of the first watch nor a different flame. Nothing passes from one to the next, and yet the second is not unconnected with the first. Continuity is designated on a succession that is neither identical nor wholly different.

A melody known by heart shows how this works. Each playing is new, and none is the previous one. There is no tune stored somewhere between playings, waiting to be released. Yet the tune is recognised across them all, and it can be played well or badly, remembered or forgotten, changed by practice. Habit, character, skill and karma are like this. Each moment is conditioned by what went before, and the link is real and reliable. But the link is designated across the moments, not carried by something that runs through them.

Two consequences follow. The first is that nothing is lost. Memory, responsibility, learning and the karmic continuity the tradition describes are all preserved, as designations that hold. The second is that nothing is fixed. A continuity not carried by an essence can bend. A habit can loosen, a character can soften, a grievance can end, because there was never anything underneath that had to stay the same. Continuity without a carrier is more reliable than the nihilist expects, and more open than the substantialist can allow.

## Chapter 9: The Candle and the Weight of Watching

Under general anaesthesia three hours pass on the clock, and none in experience. No river of time flowed by itself, carrying an unconscious passenger. On waking, the familiar self is put together again: fully functional, and nowhere to be found as a standing spectator. The continuity was not a thing that persisted through the sleep. It was designated, on waking, across the gap in experience.

This is the first thing the death chapter asks the reader to see. The fear of death is not only a fear of pain, or of leaving people behind, though those are real. It is the fear that a particular experience, the taste of this afternoon and the light in this room, will be lost, and lost to someone who has been holding it. The fear presupposes two things: that experience is a possession, and that a possessor stands behind it who can be deprived. Remove the possessor and the second half of the fear has no subject. Remove the possession and the first half has no object.

Dōgen gives the image of firewood and ash. Firewood becomes ash, and the ash cannot return to being firewood. Yet, Dōgen says, we should not regard the ash as after and the firewood as before. Each abides fully in its own position. Life does not become death, as winter does not become spring. The image removes the sense of a sequence in which one thing is lost to make way for the next.

The dread of death, in its most common form, presupposes that the vividness of experience was inherent, and that it will be taken away with the self. Remove the presupposition and the dread loses its object. The view does not promise that anything of the person continues as the person. It does not claim a carrier after all. It does not make the loss of someone we love painless. What it removes is one particular dread: the image of an experience that is held, a holder who is taken away, and a holding left unfinished.

In its place the view offers a different relation to the present. Because nothing is being held, nothing is deferred. The value of this moment does not depend on how many moments come after it, since no moment was ever a container for the rest. Attention need not hurry toward a future it cannot have, or cling backward to a past it cannot keep. The luminosity of this appearance, its clarity, its colour and the sound in the next room, is available now, including to the one who is dying. That availability was never conditional on a long life.

The candle gives exactly as much light as before. Nothing is taken from it by going out, and the room is not darker for the flame's giving. What changes is the weight with which it is watched.

For the person who thought they had a self to lose, the burning down was a countdown to catastrophe. Once the self is seen as designated, the same burning is watched differently. It is not a loss suffered by a holder. It is the fact of appearance, undivided, lit and passing. The light was never something the candle owned, so its dimming does not subtract from anyone.

The view asks nothing of the dying about what comes after. It asks them to see that the thing they were holding was never a thing, and that what remains, the vividness of this moment, has never needed a bearer to be complete.

*Notes: The firewood and ash image is Dōgen's, from the Genjōkōan of the Shōbōgenzō; the "ash is after, firewood is before" wording is from Paul Jaffe's translation (1996, in Yasutani, Flowers Fall). The anaesthesia example is from "Death and continuity". The sentence on "the weight with which it is watched" is the book's own phrasing and should stay consistent with the conclusion.*

## Part II: bridge to Part III

The logic of substance keeps returning, and it returns through the three candidates for a ground: the Given, energy, and potentiality. Each has been shown to be a designation that works but cannot stand on its own. Continuity, too, was shown to need no carrier. What remains is the question of how the mind divides appearance in the first place, and what the analysis of that division can and cannot do.
