## Appendix B: Glossary

**Abandonment.** The ending of the afflictions, possible only because they are empty.

**Afflictions** (*kleśa*). States that disturb the mind and drive suffering: craving, aversion and delusion.

**Apoha.** Exclusion. On Dharmakīrti's account, a universal is understood by excluding what it is not, rather than by pointing to a common essence.

**Avikalpa.** Non-conceptual awareness: registering what presents without the names, categories and part-whole structure that thought adds.

**Basis** (*gzhi*). In Dzogchen, the primordial state of any sentient being, from which path and fruition are said to unfold; knowledge of it is *rigpa*. Longchenpa calls it a "groundless ground": it seems to be a ground and is posited as one only from a relative perspective, while in the true nature of reality it is empty of being a ground. This book reads the basis as the emptiness of appearing, not as a ground behind appearances. See the Note on Method. *Confirmed against Longchenpa as quoted in the Wikipedia "Ground (Dzogchen)" article.*

**Clarity** (*gsal ba*). In Dzogchen, one of the three aspects of the basis, together with emptiness (*stong pa nyid*) and compassionate energy (*snying rje*). In this book, the knowing clarity of appearing itself, not a substance behind it. See Luminosity. *The three-aspect scheme is confirmed by Madhyamaka.com and Rangjung Yeshe. The book's own gloss is stated as a gloss.*

**Conditions** (*pratyaya*). What something arises in dependence on. The Abhidharma listed four kinds.

**Contrivance** (in this book). The same unfixed appearing, met with the habit of fixing still operating. Its counterpart is openness. This is a term of the book's own, not a standard Dzogchen term.

**Conventional truth** (*saṃvṛti-satya*). The level of valid designation, in which causal explanation and the words "why" and "because" are usable.

**Dependent arising** (*pratītyasamutpāda*). Things appear only in dependence on other things, and on the way they are picked out.

**Dependent designation.** Things are named and known in dependence on the conditions and the naming itself. Dependent designation is not a compromise between existence and non-existence.

**Dharmatā** (*chos nyid*). In the standard sources, the true nature of phenomena, often glossed as the intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) of dharmas, which is constant and transcends discrimination. This book uses "nature" for the way things are, when they are not reified, in a narrower sense than that gloss. It avoids the term for emptiness itself. The difference is stated where the term is first used.

**Elaboration** (*prapañca*). The mind's spinning-out of "is" and "is not" around something it takes to be there.

**Eight extremes.** Arising and ceasing, permanence and annihilation, coming and going, identity and difference.

**Emptiness** (*śūnyatā*, *stong pa nyid*). The lack of intrinsic nature. Not a separate reality behind phenomena, and not itself a hidden substrate. Emptiness has no intrinsic nature of its own, and this book does not call it open, closed or luminous in itself.

**Implicative and non-implicative negation.** Non-implicative negation (*prasajya-pratiṣedha*) denies without installing a replacement. Implicative negation (*paryudāsa*) denies and installs a positive ground in its place.

**Lhun grub.** Spontaneous presence. What dependent arising looks like once no producer is sought. Not an uncaused ground. In Higgins's account (via KMaps), spontaneous presence is the form in which the basic openness of awareness surges forth as luminosity.

**Luminosity** (*od gsal*). In Dzogchen, a term that is categorised under the clarity aspect of the nature of mind, and related to *lhun grub*. In this book, the knowing clarity of appearance, not a substance behind it. Rangjung Yeshe gives *stong pa'i 'od gsal* as "empty luminosity" and "the luminosity of emptiness." See Clarity.

**Non-arising** (*anutpāda*). No arising found from a thing's own side. This is not the same as no causes.

**Openness** (in this book). The way unfixed appearing meets when it is seen without fixing. It is not a property of emptiness or of the basis, and it does not explain why appearing occurs. Its counterpart is contrivance. This is a departure from the sources. In the sources, openness appears as a characterisation of the ground and of awareness: *rig stong* (cognitive intensity and openness) and *gsal stong* (luminosity and openness, or the unity of clarity and openness), both linked to *lhun grub*. The book's usage is narrower. *Confirmed: Higgins's usage via KMaps; Rangjung Yeshe for gsal stong.*

**Own-nature** (*svabhāva*). A nature a thing would have from its own side, apart from everything else. Defined in MMK 15.2 as non-contingent and not dependent on anything other than itself. Madhyamaka argues that nothing has it, and that emptiness is not one either. Distinguish from nature as the way things are (dharmatā). The Tibetan *rang bzhin* is used for both, and in Dzogchen also for the ultimate nature, unaltered because the basis is spontaneously accomplished. The book distinguishes these by context.

**Primordial purity** (*ka dag*). The Dzogchen description of the basis as pure from the beginning, and a way of signifying emptiness. In this book, read as the unfixed character of appearing, not as an uncaused source. *Confirmed: Higgins (via KMaps) gives "a term signifying primordial purity as a way of signifying emptiness," unique to rNying ma tantric literature. Namkhai Norbu describes the basis as "uncreated, ever pure, and self-perfected, not something that has to be constructed," which supports "unproduced" in this sense. The Buddha-Nature site glosses it as the empty nature of phenomena, and Tshig mdzod chen mo defines it as pure from the beginning, free from stain.*

**Rang grol.** Self-release: thought dissolves when it is not followed or fixed. The term is standard in Dzogchen and Mahāmudrā (Wikipedia, "Dzogchen"; Studybuddhism glossary).

**Rang rig.** Self-cognizing awareness. The Dzogchen position on it is debated: Mipham's *Beacon of Certainty* defends Dzogchen in Madhyamaka terms, and Higgins reads Longchenpa as rejecting self-reflective awareness. Chapter 18 presents these readings and the Madhyamaka objection.

**Recognition** (*ngo shes*; *rang ngo shes* for self-recognition). In Dzogchen, the central term of the path. Knowledge of the basis (*rigpa*) comes from recognising it, and recognition is often identified with awakening: the recognition of the basis is "awakening" in the Dzogchen sources as summarised by Wikipedia. This is a departure from the book's own usage. In the book, recognition is not an achievement in the sense of a state built up by effort; the Dzogchen sources treat it as the decisive accomplishment.

**Reification.** Taking an abstraction, or an appearance, to exist as a thing in its own right, standing beneath the particulars.

**Rigpa** (*rig pa*). In Dzogchen, the knowledge of the basis, and the pristine awareness that is the basis itself. Its opposite is *ma rigpa*, ignorance, the failure to recognise the basis. Sanskrit *vidyā* ("knowledge"). The book's "knowing clarity" is a gloss on this, not a translation. Ācārya Malcolm Smith translates rigpa as *vidyā*, prefers to leave it untranslated, and holds that rendering it as "awareness" is wrong (quoted in his DharmaWheel posts and the Wisdom Podcast). *Confirmed by Wikipedia's Rigpa article: "knowledge of the ground," opposite ma rigpa.*

**Svalakṣaṇa.** The particular, what characterises itself alone, in Dignāga's and Dharmakīrti's epistemology. Candrakīrti's first chapter answers a view closely resembling Dignāga's (see the JIABS study "Candrakīrti on Dignāga on Svalakṣaṇas"). See Chapter 4.

**Ultimate truth** (*paramārtha-satya*). Not a higher world, but the emptiness of the conventional one.

**Unobstructed** (*ma 'gags pa*). Nothing blocks the display, and there is no store behind it from which the display comes. Not that something lasts for ever.

**Vikalpa.** Conceptual construction: the mode of mind that divides, names and asks which part carries identity.

## Appendix C: The Beginner's Companion, Path Outline

The Beginner's Companion is written as a series of rounds. The narrative chapters carry the argument, and the Companion gives a reader who wants a step-by-step path a way through it. The outline below maps each round to the chapters it supports.

- **Round 1: The observer.** Look for the witness behind seeing and hearing. Chapters 1 and 2.
- **Round 2: Vividness without a viewer.** Notice that experience is vivid without a holder. Chapter 2.
- **Round 3: The chariot and the parts.** Apply the chariot to the observer and to objects. Chapters 1 and 13.
- **Round 4: Dependence and designation.** The Dharmakīrti and Candrakīrti exchange, with the rope and snake. Chapters 4 and 5.
- **Round 5: The Given and its alternatives.** The two-storey picture, the Given, and conceptual all the way down. Chapters 4, 5 and 14.
- **Round 6: Energy, potentiality and continuity.** Test each candidate for a carrier. Chapters 6, 7 and 8.
- **Round 7: Change without a changer.** Look for the changer apart from the change. Chapter 10.
- **Round 8: The eight extremes.** Apply each extreme to a single object. Chapter 11.
- **Round 9: Analysis and seeing.** Use the dividing mind against itself, then rest in non-dividing seeing. Chapters 13 and 15.
- **Round 10: The taste.** Notice seen as just seen, heard as just heard. Chapter 20.
- **Round 11: The meal.** The painted food and painted hunger exercise. Chapter 21.

*Check: the round numbering and titles are provisional. The Companion's own rounds, which are referred to in the manuscript inserts as Rounds Four and Five, need to be aligned with this outline before publication.*
